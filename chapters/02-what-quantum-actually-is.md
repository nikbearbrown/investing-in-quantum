# Chapter 2 — What Quantum Actually Is (And Isn't)

## Decision question

You keep seeing the same words — *qubit*, *superposition*, *entanglement*, *fidelity* — in every quantum press release, every analyst note, every breathless headline. You do not want a physics degree. You want to know exactly this: **What do I actually need to understand about quantum physics to evaluate an investment claim — and to tell a real advance from a marketing one?**

## The short answer

Three physical concepts and six investor terms. The three concepts are *superposition* (a qubit holds a blend of 0 and 1 while it computes), *entanglement* (qubits can be correlated in ways no classical system can reproduce), and *decoherence* (the environment destroys those quantum states, which is why building the machines is hard). The six terms — qubit versus logical qubit, physical versus logical error rate, gate fidelity, coherence time, and BQP versus BPP — are the vocabulary a credible claim must be stateable in. If a press release cannot be restated in these terms, it is probably marketing. That is the whole chapter, and the rest is detail.

## Why this matters

Almost every expensive mistake in this sector traces to a vocabulary failure. An investor who cannot tell a *physical* qubit from a *logical* one will be impressed by "1,000 qubits!" — a number that, on its own, says nothing about whether the machine can do anything useful. An investor who does not know that *gate fidelity* compounds will not understand why the gap between 99.9% and 99.99% is not a rounding difference but, by one hardware maker's own estimate, a factor of ten billion in end-to-end performance over a long computation.

The consequence is concrete: you will pay for the wrong number. The industry itself has lived through this. For years, qubit count was the headline metric, and companies raced to announce ever-larger chips. Then the field publicly repudiated the metric — pivoting, in the words of practitioners, "from more qubits to better qubits" — because most of those qubits were too noisy and short-lived to run a useful algorithm ([Fowler, 2024](https://gafowler.medium.com/the-big-shift-from-more-qubits-to-better-qubits-76c2c3cecb32)). An investor still anchored on qubit count in 2026 is using a metric the engineers abandoned. The vocabulary in this chapter is your defense: it lets you read a claim, find the numbers that matter, and notice when they are conspicuously absent.

## The framework

No equations. Three concepts, then six terms — each defined in plain language with its investment relevance attached.

**Superposition — the resource, not the magic.** A classical bit is either 0 or 1. A qubit, *while it is computing*, holds a weighted blend of both at once: a superposition. The popular shorthand — "a qubit tries all answers in parallel" — is the single most damaging misconception in the field, because it implies free, unlimited parallelism. The truth is stricter and more interesting. The blend is described by *amplitudes*, which (unlike ordinary probabilities) can be negative, and the only way to get an answer out is to *measure* — which collapses the whole superposition to a single 0 or 1. The art of every quantum algorithm is arranging *interference* so that the amplitudes of wrong answers cancel out and the right answer reinforces *before* you measure. You do not read out all the branches. You read out exactly one, and the algorithm's entire job was to load the dice so that the one you get is likely the right one. A useful analogy: superposition plus interference is less like parallel universes and more like noise-cancelling headphones — engineered cancellation of unwanted waves so the signal you want survives. The double-slit experiment, in which a single particle's amplitude passes through both slits and interferes with itself, is superposition made visible; quantum computing harnesses that same self-interference deliberately ([introtoquantum.org](https://introtoquantum.org/essentials/myths/)).

**Entanglement — "spooky action," but useful.** When two qubits become entangled, their states are correlated in a way that no separate classical description can reproduce: measure one, and you instantly fix what you will find on the other, however far apart they are. Einstein called this "spooky action at a distance." For the investor, skip the philosophy and keep the capacity: entanglement is what lets *n* qubits encode correlations that would take 2-to-the-*n* classical numbers to write down, and it is a *necessary* ingredient for any genuine quantum speedup. No entanglement, no advantage. One myth to retire on sight: entanglement does *not* let you send information faster than light — you cannot control which outcome you observe, so no usable signal travels. That fact alone deflates most "quantum communication" hype.

**Decoherence — the enemy, and the entire engineering problem.** Superposition and entanglement are exquisitely fragile. Stray heat, a vibration, a stray photon — any uncontrolled interaction with the environment — leaks the quantum information out and collapses the state. This is *decoherence*. The length of time a qubit stays usefully quantum is its *coherence time*; today's best run from microseconds into the low-millisecond range, depending heavily on the hardware. Every operation must finish well inside that window, so coherence time and gate speed together cap how deep a computation can run before noise wins. Decoherence is *why* these machines need cryogenic refrigerators, vacuum chambers, and elaborate error correction — and why building them is genuinely hard ([Preskill, 2018](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1801.00862)).

Now the six terms.

**1–2. Qubit versus logical qubit.** A *physical* qubit is the actual hardware device. A *logical* qubit is an error-corrected abstraction built out of many physical qubits, arranged so that the collective survives even though its individual parts keep failing. The intuition is a RAID array: many unreliable disks combined into one reliable volume. The catch is the exchange rate — getting one good logical qubit can cost hundreds to thousands of physical ones. So "1,000 physical qubits" might be *zero* logical qubits if the error rates are poor. The investor-relevant count is logical qubits, almost never physical.

**3–4. Physical versus logical error rate.** The *physical* error rate is how often a single hardware operation fails — today roughly one in a thousand (10⁻³), and physicists doubt it will drop much below about one in ten thousand (10⁻⁴) per operation through hardware alone. The *logical* error rate is how often the protected, encoded qubit fails. Useful, deep algorithms need logical error rates of at least one in a million (10⁻⁶), and the hardest problems want far lower. The landmark result of 2024 was that error correction now works *in the right direction*: Google showed that adding physical qubits to a logical qubit drove the logical error rate *down*, crossing the "below-threshold" line — the first convincing demonstration that the scheme scales ([Quanta Magazine, 2024](https://www.quantamagazine.org/quantum-computers-cross-critical-error-threshold-20241209/)).

**5. Gate fidelity.** A *gate* is one elementary operation on one or two qubits; *fidelity* is how accurately it does what it should. 99.9% is "three nines"; 99.99% is "four nines." Fidelity compounds like interest in reverse: a long circuit multiplies thousands of gate fidelities together, so a small per-step difference explodes. By IonQ's own estimate, the gap between three-nines and four-nines fidelity can amount to a roughly ten-billion-fold difference in end-to-end performance over a deep circuit ([Quantum Computing Report, 2025](https://quantumcomputingreport.com/ionq-achieves-99-99-two-qubit-gate-fidelity-using-electronic-qubit-control-technology/)). Higher native fidelity also *shrinks* the physical-to-logical overhead — fewer physical qubits per logical qubit to hit a 10⁻⁶ target. That is why IonQ's 99.99% two-qubit gate result in 2025 mattered. The load-bearing caveat: that figure is a record on an individual two-qubit gate, *not* a demonstration that four-nines fidelity holds across a whole machine. Whenever you see "four nines," ask: on what, and at what scale? Do not let a single-pair record be read as system-wide performance.

**6. BQP versus BPP — why advantage is problem-specific.** This is the complexity-theory bedrock, and it explains why quantum computers are not simply "faster computers." Roughly: BPP is the set of problems classical computers can solve efficiently; BQP is the set quantum computers can solve efficiently. It is *believed* — but, importantly, **not proven** — that BQP contains problems outside BPP (integer factoring is the poster child) [contested — see pantry flag: BQP≠BPP is conjectural]. State the quantum advantage as well-evidenced conjecture, not settled fact. What *is* known is that BPP sits inside BQP: a quantum computer can do everything a classical one can. But only for a *narrow, structured* set of problems does it do so dramatically faster. Quantum advantage is not general. It is a short list — and Chapter 3 gives you the filter for reading that list.

## What it looks like in practice

Apply the vocabulary as an audit. Take a generic announcement: *"Company X unveils 1,000-qubit quantum computer, a world record."* Now restate it in the six terms and watch the gaps appear. How many *logical* qubits? (Unstated.) Physical *versus* logical error rate? (Unstated.) Coherence time? (Unstated.) Gate fidelity *at that scale*? (Unstated.) Four critical numbers are missing; the only number provided — physical qubit count — is the one the engineers abandoned. Verdict: marketing.

Now contrast Google's 2024 below-threshold result, which is what a Layer-1-credible claim reads like: a *logical* error rate that *fell* as physical qubits were added, on a documented experiment with transparent methodology, reported in *Nature* and discussed by independent experts ([Quanta Magazine, 2024](https://www.quantamagazine.org/quantum-computers-cross-critical-error-threshold-20241209/)). It names the right quantity (logical error rate), shows the right direction (down with scale), and survives outside scrutiny. The difference between the two announcements is not subtle once you have the vocabulary — and invisible without it.

A second worked contrast sharpens the point about fidelity compounding, because it is the least intuitive of the six terms. Imagine two machines: Machine A runs gates at 99.9% fidelity (three nines), Machine B at 99.99% (four nines). On a single gate the difference looks trivial — a tenth of a percent. But a useful algorithm is not a single gate; it is a long chain of them. Run a circuit of ten thousand gates and the probability that *every* gate succeeds is roughly 0.999 raised to the ten-thousandth power on Machine A — which works out to a vanishingly small chance the whole computation is clean — versus 0.9999 to the ten-thousandth on Machine B, which remains workable. The same per-step gap that looked like a rounding error becomes the difference between a machine that can run the algorithm and one that cannot. This is why the field obsesses over the last decimal place of fidelity, and why "we improved fidelity from 99.9% to 99.99%" is a genuine headline while "we added 500 more qubits" frequently is not. Fidelity is the term that most rewards an investor who actually understands it.

## What to watch for

Genuine technical progress, stated in the right terms:

- **Logical error rates trending toward 10⁻⁶ and below** — on real machines, not in simulation or projection.
- **Coherence times extending into the milliseconds across full processor arrays**, not just on a single hand-picked qubit.
- **Gate fidelity above 99.99% demonstrated *at scale*** — across many qubits in a working processor, with the scope stated explicitly. The phrase "at scale" is the whole point; a two-qubit record is a starting line, not a finish.

## What to ignore

- **Physical qubit counts quoted without logical error rates.** The field abandoned this metric; so should you.
- **"World record" announcements that do not specify what was measured and by whom.** A record at what — fidelity, count, coherence, logical qubits? Independently verified, or self-reported?
- **Comparisons to classical computers that do not name the specific problem and the specific classical baseline.** "Faster than a supercomputer" is meaningless without "at what task, against which classical method."
- **Any claim that does not distinguish physical from logical qubits.** This single distinction screens out a large fraction of hype on its own.

## The decision rule

Learn these three concepts and six terms, and use them as a filter on every quantum announcement you encounter: if a claim cannot be restated in this vocabulary — naming logical qubits, error rates, fidelity, coherence, and the specific problem — treat it as marketing until proven otherwise. Read this chapter once; refer back to it as needed.
