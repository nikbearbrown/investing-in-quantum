# Chapter 12 — The 2030 Investment Horizon

## Decision question

You have heard, probably more than once, that quantum computing will "arrive" sometime around 2030. The number is everywhere: in vendor roadmaps, in consultancy reports, in the headline of the article that brought you here. So you want to know the obvious thing. When does quantum computing actually become investable on its merits rather than on its story, and what are you supposed to do with your position between now and then? This chapter answers that question — but not in the form you expect, because the honest answer is not a date.

## The short answer

There is no defensible single date, and any source that gives you one is selling a plan, not making a forecast. The disciplined approach is to map the next decade into four phases, each defined by *what you would have to observe* before moving up, and to keep your position sized for the phase you are actually in — which, as of mid-2026, is the first one: bookmark only.

## Why this matters

Getting the timeline wrong is the most expensive mistake available to a quantum investor, and it can be made in two opposite directions.

Buy too early and size too large, and you finance a decade of dilution. Quantum hardware companies burn cash. They will raise money repeatedly between now and any commercial payoff, and each raise dilutes existing holders. If the payoff is genuinely in the 2030s, a large position taken in 2026 is not "early conviction" — it is a low-interest loan to a company that will issue more shares against your stake every eighteen months. The technology can succeed and you can still lose money, simply because you paid the holding cost for a decade.

Wait too long and dismiss the sector as perpetual vaporware, and you risk being absent when a genuine breakthrough reprices everything in a quarter. The repricing in a real technology transition is fast, discontinuous, and impossible to chase once it begins.

The reason this is so hard is structural, and it is worth naming plainly: this is an immature field, and immature fields are exactly where confident predictions fail most reliably. In *The Signal and the Noise*, Nate Silver shows that the forecasters who do best are "foxes" — people who hold many partial models, update constantly, and distrust their own certainty — while "hedgehogs," who commit to one big idea and defend it, are reliably overconfident (Silver, 2012). The single most important property of a good forecast, Silver argues, is *calibration*: when you say something is 40 percent likely, it should happen about 40 percent of the time. Quantum-timeline forecasts are almost universally uncalibrated, because the people making them have an incentive — equity, grants, reputation — to sound certain. The framework below is an attempt to be a fox in a field full of hedgehogs.

## The framework

The book uses four phases. **Two cautions, stated up front and not buried.** First, the *boundary years* — 2028, 2032, 2035 — are this book's own organizing scaffold, not a consensus forecast and not a prediction. They are convenient labels for "the period in which a certain kind of milestone becomes plausible," nothing more. Treat them the way you would treat chapter dividers, not deadlines. Second, what defines each phase is not the calendar but the *signals* — the four-part breakthrough signal developed in Chapter 8 (a real chemical-accuracy result on a hard molecule; that result surviving classical counterattack; sustained sovereign procurement revenue; and a major-incumbent acquisition such as NVIDIA buying a hardware company). You move phases when signals fire, whatever the year on the calendar.

**Phase 1 (now to roughly 2028) — hardware science and the moving baseline.** This is where we are. The work being done is real and is genuinely accelerating — multiple platforms have now demonstrated below-threshold error correction, meaning their logical qubits get *better*, not worse, as you add physical qubits. But there is no commercially meaningful quantum advantage anywhere, and the revenue that exists (for example, IonQ's roughly $130M in 2025) is hardware sales, cloud access, and government procurement — a floor, not a fundamentals story. Two things define this phase for the investor. The revenue floor comes from sovereign procurement (Chapter 9): governments buying optionality because they cannot afford to be unprepared. And the *baseline is moving against quantum*: classical AI is accelerating drug discovery and materials science right now, which means the bar quantum eventually has to clear keeps rising. Action: bookmark position only.

**Phase 2 (roughly 2028 to 2032) — first credible fault-tolerant demonstrations.** Here the question shifts from "can they keep a logical qubit alive?" to "can they do something useful with a few of them?" The marker to watch for is an early chemical-accuracy result on a *small transition-metal complex* — a molecule classical methods struggle with — where the classical comparison is honestly specified and quantum wins. This is a *target*, not an achieved milestone; no 2026 result meets it. If a credible, independently reproduced demonstration of this kind appears and survives scrutiny, Signals 1 and 2 begin to fire. Action: modest increase from bookmark, not a re-rating.

**Phase 3 (roughly 2032 to 2035) — first economically important advantage.** This is where the thesis either earns its keep or doesn't. If quantum simulation delivers a result of real economic value — a catalyst, a battery material, a drug candidate evaluated more accurately than any classical method can — and *all four signals have fired*, the repricing of quantum equities will be dramatic and fast. Action: meaningful position, but only on confirmed signals.

**Phase 4 (post-2035) — quantum as infrastructure.** If molecular simulation delivers, counterfactual drug design and novel-materials discovery become commercially real, and quantum stops being a speculative position and becomes infrastructure, the way cloud computing did. Action: treat as infrastructure, not speculation.

**The pessimistic scenario — required, not optional.** A handbook that gave you only the four-phase path would be dishonest by omission. There is a serious, minority scientific position that fault-tolerant quantum computing at useful scale may never be achieved, and it deserves to be presented fairly rather than as a fringe footnote.

Gil Kalai, a mathematician at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem and Reichman University, argues that the noise in entangled quantum systems is *correlated* in a way that scales with the computation itself, so that error rates cannot in practice be pushed below the threshold that fault-tolerance theory requires. His is a complexity-theoretic argument — a claim about a wall built into the structure of the problem, not an engineering inconvenience to be solved with better hardware (Kalai, arXiv:1908.02499, "The Argument against Quantum Computers," and his ongoing work). It is important to understand that Kalai is not a crank: he understands fault-tolerance theory deeply, and Scott Aaronson — who thinks Kalai is wrong — has debated him respectfully for roughly fifteen years.

Michel Dyakonov, a physicist, makes a different argument: that a many-qubit state is described by an enormous number of *continuous* parameters, which makes a quantum computer an irreducibly *analog* device, and no analog device has ever been made fault-tolerant. This is a control-and-precision objection. (We did not surface a fresh 2024–2026 Dyakonov publication, so we present his standing, established argument rather than a current statement.)

[contested — see pantry flag] The recent below-threshold results, especially demonstrations across dozens of logical qubits, are exactly the kind of evidence that *pressures* Kalai's conjecture. His reply is that the wall appears only at larger scales and longer circuits than anyone has yet run. As of mid-2026 the dispute is not settled in either direction. The point for the investor is simple: the bookmark position is sized correctly for the world in which the pessimists turn out to be right. It costs you little if they are.

## What it looks like in practice

Consider how to read a roadmap. IBM's public plan describes a system called Starling around 2029 — on the order of 200 logical qubits and 100 million gate operations — followed by a far larger system in the 2033-and-beyond range. The instinct is to treat these dates as predictions. They are not. They are a vendor's plan, which is Tier-3 evidence in the credibility hierarchy of the next chapter. The correct use of a roadmap date is to convert it into a *test*: "By around 2029, IBM intends to demonstrate roughly 200 logical qubits. Has an independent party confirmed that this milestone was reached, and was it useful?" The roadmap is a hypothesis the company is offering about itself. Your job is to wait for the independent confirmation, not to price in the plan.

Now consider the forecaster spread, which is itself the most useful data point in the chapter. IBM's roadmap clusters first-generation fault tolerance around 2029 and larger systems around 2033. McKinsey and BCG analyses tend to cluster *commercial* advantage around 2035 — one widely cited survey found only about 72 percent of executives expected commercial fault-tolerant computing by 2035, which means roughly a quarter did not. Other segmentations push broad, full-scale fault tolerance past 2040. So credible sources disagree by more than a decade. That disagreement is not noise to be averaged away into a tidy "2032." It is the signal. When serious forecasters spread across a decade, the honest investor stops betting on the date and starts gating on the milestone. (Treat the specific dollar market-size figures in consultancy reports as the *least* reliable input of all — they are Tier-3 projections multiplied by Tier-3 adoption assumptions.)

## What to watch for

- **Independent reproduction of a fault-tolerant demonstration.** Not a vendor press release — a result that a second, unaffiliated group confirms. This is the Phase-2 trigger.
- **A chemical-accuracy result on a real transition-metal complex** with an honestly specified classical comparison. This is the scientific half of the four-part signal.
- **The classical counterattack clock.** When a quantum chemistry result appears, watch how long it takes a group like the Flatiron Institute to match it classically. A result that survives 12 to 24 months is meaningfully different from one matched in weeks.
- **Sovereign procurement converting to recurring revenue**, not one-off grants — the floor becoming a fundamentals story for a specific company.

## What to ignore

- **Any single phase-boundary year quoted as a fact.** Including the ones in this chapter. They are scaffolding.
- **Vendor roadmap dates treated as forecasts.** They are plans; convert them into tests.
- **Consultancy dollar figures for the 2035 quantum market.** Built on stacked assumptions, each individually shaky.
- **A breakthrough headline before the classical counterattack has run.** A claim of advantage that no independent group has yet tried to match classically is an untested hypothesis, not a milestone.
- **The argument that "this time it's different" because progress feels fast.** Below-threshold error correction is genuine progress, but it does not collapse the timeline to useful, economically important advantage. Speed in the lab is not speed to market.

## The decision rule

Stay in the phase you can actually observe, not the one you hope for. Phase 1 → bookmark only. Phase 2, with a credible, independently reproduced demonstration (Signals 1 and 2) → modest increase. Phase 3, with all four signals fired → meaningful position. Phase 4 → treat as infrastructure. When someone hands you a date, replace it in your head with the question: *what would I have to see?* — and do not move until you have seen it.

## Further reading

- **Nate Silver, *The Signal and the Noise: Why So Many Predictions Fail — but Some Don't*** (Penguin, 2012) — the accessible source for why confident point-forecasts in immature fields fail and why calibrated, fox-like, signal-gated thinking beats them; read it as the methodological backbone of this chapter.
- **John Preskill, "Quantum Computing in the NISQ Era and Beyond," *Quantum* 2 (2018): 79** — the independent academic statement of why current machines are *not* fault-tolerant and what it would take to change that, from the physicist who named the era we are in.
- **Gil Kalai, "The Argument against Quantum Computers," arXiv:1908.02499 (2019)** — the primary source for the pessimistic scenario, written by the most serious skeptic in the field; read it to understand exactly which experimental result would have to appear to refute him.
