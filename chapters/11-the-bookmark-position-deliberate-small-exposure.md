# Chapter 11 — The Bookmark Position: Deliberate Small Exposure

## Decision question

You have read enough to know that quantum computing is real, that the timeline is long, that most of the commercial narrative is wrong, and that the breakthrough signal that would justify a serious bet has not fired. So here is the awkward question that follows from all of it: **if you cannot responsibly bet on quantum yet, why own any of it at all — and if the answer is "a little," then what, exactly, and how much?**

## The short answer

Own a tiny position — one to ten shares, or a dollar amount you would not miss — in one or more of the public hardware names, plus whatever ambient exposure you already have through IBM and Alphabet. The position is not a financial bet. It is an *epistemic* instrument: a deliberate device for improving the quality of your attention. It must be small enough to pass one test — that you are genuinely fine if it goes to zero.

## Why this matters

This is the chapter where the handbook's entire thesis turns into an action, and the action is counterintuitive enough that most readers get it wrong by instinct. The instinct says: if a thing is worth owning, own a meaningful amount; conviction means size. That instinct is correct for most investments. It is wrong here, and understanding *why* it is wrong is the whole point.

The cost of getting this wrong runs in two directions. Size the position too large, and you have made a speculative bet on a pre-revenue technology whose timeline is measured in years-to-decades and whose every benchmark claim is suspect — and worse, you have corrupted the very judgment you need to navigate the years ahead (more on that mechanism below). Size it at zero, and you remain a passive, lazy watcher of a sector you have decided is worth watching — which means you will not actually watch it well, and you will arrive at the moment the signal fires under-informed, reacting to a price move you do not understand.

The bookmark position threads this. It is engineered to give you the attention benefits of ownership while removing the financial pressure that would distort your thinking. It is, in the most literal sense, a position whose return is measured in *understanding*, not dollars.

## The framework

**The bookmark as attention technology.** Humans track what they own. This is not a flaw to be corrected; it is a lever to be used. The moment you own a single share of a company, abstract "quantum news" becomes "news about my thing," and you start to read the earnings call you would have skipped, notice the fidelity number you would have glossed over, and absorb the domain knowledge this book wants you building across the long window between now and the 2030s. The dollar amount is deliberately trivial. The position is a *bookmark* — a placeholder that holds your attention on the page. Five shares of a quantum stock is lunch money; it still buys you a quarterly reason to open the filing and learn what "two-qubit gate fidelity" means. The purpose of the trade is not return. It is to install a standing reason to pay attention.

**The "fine if it goes to zero" test — the confirmation-bias constraint.** This is the section to read twice. Skin in the game sharpens attention, but *too much* skin corrupts it, and it does so through a well-documented mechanism. Once a position is large enough that losing it would hurt, the owner stops being a neutral observer and becomes an advocate. You begin, without noticing, to seek out evidence that confirms your holding was smart and to dismiss evidence that says otherwise. This is motivated reasoning, and in a field where the honest answer to most questions is "we don't know yet," it is poison.

It helps to borrow a distinction from Kahneman, Sibony, and Sunstein's *Noise* (2021). They separate two kinds of error. *Noise* is random scatter — different judges, different days, different moods, producing different answers to the same question. *Bias* is systematic error in one direction — the bent gun sight that pulls every shot the same way. Your judgment about a pre-revenue, hype-saturated, scientifically contested field is *already* noisy; that is unavoidable, and it is why this book insists on frameworks and external authorities like the Flatiron Institute rather than your own gut. What you can control is the *bias*. A position sized large enough to hurt is a bent gun sight: it pulls every one of your quantum judgments systematically toward "my holding is fine." The bookmark is engineered to keep that bias near zero, so that your already-noisy judgment at least stays *unbiased* — pointed at the truth, even if it scatters around it.

The test is operational, not philosophical. Imagine the stock dropping 50 percent overnight. If your honest reaction would be curiosity — "what happened, what did I miss, what does this tell me about the thesis?" — the position is correctly sized. If your honest reaction would be the urge to defend the company, to post on a forum explaining why the market is wrong, to add more to "average down" before you have checked whether anything fundamental changed — the position is too large. If you cannot say "I am fine if this goes to zero" and mean it, shrink it until you can. Taleb's *Fooled by Randomness* (2001) supplies the companion warning: a single pure-play's stock moves are mostly chance relative to the slow grind of the underlying science, and the surest way to be fooled is to let a position large enough to matter trick you into reading random price action as meaningful signal.

**Bookmark versus bet versus ambient exposure.** Three distinct relationships, and you want to be in two of them. A *bet* is sized to matter; it demands a thesis and an exit, and this book argues you have no business making one until the four-part signal fires (Chapter 8). A *bookmark* is sized to be financially ignorable but attentionally unignorable. *Ambient exposure* is quantum you already own without choosing it — most readers holding a broad index fund already own IBM and Alphabet, both of which have real quantum programs. The claim of this chapter is that the correct posture, right now, is bookmark plus ambient, never bet.

## What it looks like in practice

The companies worth bookmarking, with the figures that matter — all of which move and should be re-checked [verify]:

- **IonQ (NYSE: IONQ)** — trapped-ion design, publicly traded, the clearest bookmark candidate because its disclosures are the richest. IonQ reported roughly $130 million in revenue for fiscal 2025 (up sharply year over year), making it the first quantum company to clear $100 million in revenue [verify]. It has pursued an aggressive acquisition strategy — Oxford Ionics (roughly $1.075 billion, closed September 2025) and SkyWater Technology (roughly $1.8 billion, announced January 2026) [verify]. One caution belongs in bold: **$130 million in revenue is a milestone of commercial *traction* — hardware sales, cloud access, government contracts — not evidence of fault-tolerant quantum *advantage*.** Do not let the headline number trick you into thinking the science has been validated. It has not.
- **Quantinuum** — trapped-ion design, Honeywell-backed, with among the highest two-qubit gate fidelities in the industry [verify exact figure and metric]. An initial public offering was anticipated for 2026; by the time you read this it may have priced or closed, so check its status before assuming you cannot buy it [verify — status may have advanced]. Bookmark it when shares become accessible.
- **QuEra** — neutral-atom design, the technical pace-setter, holder of the logical-qubit record (96 logical qubits from 448 physical atoms, published in *Nature*, January 2026), backed by a round led by Google's quantum group alongside SoftBank and NVIDIA's venture arm. **QuEra is private. It cannot be bought as a stock.** Watch it as the company setting the technical pace; do not imagine a retail bookmark is possible there.

And the exposure you may already have, requiring no action at all:

- **IBM (NYSE: IBM)** — superconducting design, the deepest quantum software ecosystem, with a below-threshold quantum-memory milestone on its Heron-class processors.
- **Alphabet / Google (NASDAQ: GOOGL)** — superconducting design, the Willow chip's error-correction milestone, and a venture arm active in the sector.

If you hold a broad US index fund, you already own slivers of both. That is ambient quantum exposure you did not choose — and it may already exceed what you would deliberately buy.

**The stay-away list, with the specific reason each fails:**

- **Companies whose revenue story depends on near-term commercial applications of today's noisy machines** (the "NISQ-era" narrative). The hardware is not ready for commercially meaningful use; revenue built on it is research spending and early-access fees dressed as a product.
- **Companies claiming they will accelerate AI or "speed up large language models."** This thesis is not premature — it is wrong, dismantled by the dequantization literature (Chapter 4). A company leaning on it either does not understand its own hardware's limits or is marketing to investors who do not.
- **Rigetti (RGTI)** — superconducting, but with no clear differentiation from far larger and better-resourced players like IBM and Google, and a valuation untethered from its very small revenue [verify figures]. Stay away.
- **D-Wave (NYSE: QBTS)** — built on quantum *annealing*, a design whose claimed advantage is contested and has been challenged by classical simulation (Chapter 6 covers the counterattack pattern). It trades at a high valuation on small revenue [verify]. Stay away as a quantum-*advantage* bet.

Both Rigetti and D-Wave are the same pattern: a price set for a future the science has not delivered.

## What to watch for

- **Logical error rate improving** — not raw physical qubit count, which is close to meaningless on its own.
- **Sovereign procurement contracts with specific deliverables and multi-year terms** (Chapter 9), as opposed to memoranda of understanding and press releases about "interest."
- **NVIDIA deepening its stakes** or, the big one, making a controlling acquisition (Chapter 10).
- **Architecture consolidation** — signs that one hardware design is pulling decisively ahead.

## What to ignore

- **The price action itself, day to day.** A pure-play quantum stock can double or halve on sentiment in a quarter while the science moves not at all. Reading those moves as signal is exactly the error the bookmark size is meant to immunize you against.
- **Revenue headlines presented as scientific validation.** Traction is not advantage.
- **Anyone telling you that owning "a meaningful amount" shows conviction.** Here, conviction is epistemic, not financial. The conviction is in the attention, not the dollars.

## The decision rule

Open bookmark positions now in the accessible names — IonQ today, Quantinuum when its shares are buyable — and recognize the ambient exposure you likely already hold through IBM and Alphabet. Keep each position to one to ten shares, or a dollar amount you would shrug off entirely. Apply the "fine if it goes to zero" test honestly and shrink anything that fails it. Do not average down on bad news, and do not size up, until the four-part breakthrough signal fires (Chapter 8). The bookmark is the correct action for this phase of the thesis — and the whole point of keeping it small is that it keeps your judgment clean enough to recognize the moment it is no longer the right size.

## Further reading

- **Daniel Kahneman, Olivier Sibony, and Cass Sunstein, *Noise: A Flaw in Human Judgment* (2021)** — accessible treatment of the bias-versus-noise distinction that underwrites the "fine if it goes to zero" test; the chapters on motivated reasoning explain why a position large enough to hurt corrupts judgment.
- **Independent behavioral-finance literature on the disposition effect and motivated reasoning** — the academic basis for why owners of a losing position rationalize rather than reassess, the precise failure the bookmark constraint is designed to prevent.
- **Company filings: IonQ's SEC reports and Quantinuum's IPO prospectus (2025–2026)** — the primary sources; read them yourself to practice distinguishing revenue traction from scientific advantage, and to see how a quantum company describes its own fidelity numbers before a third party verifies them.
