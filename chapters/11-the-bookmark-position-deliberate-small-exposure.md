# Chapter 11 — The Bookmark Position: Deliberate Small Exposure

*Why the right size for a position you believe in might be one you would not miss.*

There is a paradox at the center of this chapter, and it is worth stating plainly before anything else. You have spent ten chapters building a framework for evaluating quantum computing claims. You have learned to read a fidelity number, to spot the difference between a physical qubit and a logical one, to recognize when a press release is describing an estimate rather than a result. You understand why the four-part breakthrough signal has not fired. You know the timeline is years to decades. You are, in short, an informed skeptic — and the chapter that follows all of that is going to tell you to buy a little anyway.

The paradox is not a contradiction. It resolves as soon as you understand what the position is actually for.

---

## What a bookmark does

Humans track what they own. This is not a cognitive flaw to be corrected; it is a feature to be used deliberately. The moment you own a single share of a company, abstract news about a sector becomes news about your thing. You open the earnings call you would have skipped. You notice the fidelity number you would have glossed over. You accumulate, slowly and almost involuntarily, the domain knowledge this book is trying to give you — because you now have a standing reason to pay attention every quarter for the next decade.

The quantum computing story will not resolve quickly. The timeline from the current hardware state to the fault-tolerant machines that Signal 1 requires runs to years, probably the early 2030s at the earliest. Staying genuinely informed across that window — not just remembering that quantum exists, but actually tracking the technical progress with enough granularity to recognize when something real happens — requires a sustained attention that most people cannot manufacture on demand. The bookmark position installs it.

Five shares of a quantum stock is lunch money. It still buys you a quarterly reason to open the filing, look at the coherence time figures, and ask whether the logical error rate is moving in the right direction. The dollar amount is deliberately trivial. The return the position is meant to generate is understanding, not yield.

---

## The corruption mechanism

Here is the problem the position size is designed to prevent, and it is worth understanding precisely because it operates invisibly.

Once a position is large enough that losing it would hurt, you stop being a neutral observer and become an advocate. You begin — without noticing, because the mechanism is subconscious — to read evidence selectively. Confirming news feels conclusive. Disconfirming news feels like it has nuances the market is missing. You seek out analysts who agree with your position. You dismiss the Flatiron Institute's classical counterattack as "they always do this." You find reasons to average down before you have checked whether anything fundamental changed.

This is motivated reasoning, and in a field where the honest answer to most technical questions is genuinely uncertain, it is dangerous. The problem is not that you are being dishonest. The problem is that a large position bends the gun sight — it introduces a systematic bias into every quantum judgment you make going forward. Your assessments were already noisy before you owned anything; that is the irreducible uncertainty of a contested frontier technology. What the position size controls is the *direction* of the scatter. A position small enough that you are genuinely fine if it goes to zero keeps your judgments unbiased — pointed at the truth, even if they scatter around it. A position large enough to hurt pulls every judgment the same way, and that way is toward "my holding is fine."

The test is operational. Imagine the stock drops fifty percent overnight. If your honest first reaction is curiosity — what happened, what did I miss, does this change the thesis? — the position is correctly sized. If your honest first reaction is the urge to defend the company, to explain why the market is wrong, to add more before you have checked anything — the position is too large. Shrink it until the curiosity reaction is genuine. That is the bookmark size.

## Bookmark versus bet versus ambient exposure

These are three distinct relationships to quantum, and the framework calls for two of them.

A *bet* is sized to matter. It demands a thesis and an exit. This book's argument is that you have no business making one until the four-part signal fires — and as of mid-2026, it has not. A bet made now is a bet on a timeline and a magnitude that are both genuinely uncertain, with capital that is actually at risk, managed by judgment that will be corrupted by the position's size. The chapters on fault-tolerant hardware, molecular simulation, and the breakthrough signal exist precisely to explain why that bet is premature.

A *bookmark* is sized to be financially ignorable but attentionally unignorable. The dollar amount you would shrug off entirely; the attention it installs is the point.

*Ambient exposure* is quantum you already own without having chosen it. Most readers holding a broad US index fund already own IBM and Alphabet, both of which have real quantum hardware programs — IBM with its Heron-class superconducting processors and a below-threshold quantum-memory milestone, Alphabet with the Willow chip's error-correction result and an active venture arm in the sector. You may already have more quantum exposure than you realize, in which case the deliberate bookmark is smaller than you thought, and the ambient position handles much of what it needs to handle.

The correct posture right now is bookmark plus ambient. Never bet — not yet.

## The stay-away list

Two names belong here specifically, not as permanent verdicts but as illustrations of the pattern to avoid.

**Rigetti** (RGTI) is a superconducting design competing directly against IBM and Google — both of which have vastly larger resources, deeper software ecosystems, and more credible technical roadmaps. Rigetti's revenue is very small relative to its valuation [verify]. The position is priced for a future the science has not delivered, against competitors whose scale makes differentiation difficult to sustain.

**D-Wave** (NYSE: QBTS) is built on quantum annealing, an architecture whose claimed advantage has been challenged by classical simulation and remains contested [contested]. The theoretical basis for advantage in annealing is weaker than in gate-based quantum computing, and the specific results D-Wave has cited have attracted classical counterattacks of the type covered in Chapter 6. It trades at a valuation that reflects commercial quantum advantage; the science does not yet support that valuation.

The pattern both names share: a stock price set for a future the underlying technology has not yet produced, in a sector where benchmark claims are routinely forgeable and classical counterattacks arrive faster than most investors expect. Stay away — not forever, but until the four-part signal or the specific company's technical story changes substantively.

A broader category also belongs on the avoid list: any company whose revenue story depends on commercial applications of today's noisy, pre-fault-tolerant machines. The hardware is not ready for commercially meaningful use. Revenue built on it is research spending and early-access fees. That is real money — IonQ's $130 million is not fabricated — but it is not evidence of the thing you are waiting for. Any company telling you it will accelerate AI or speed up large language models on today's quantum hardware is either wrong about the physics or is marketing to investors who have not read the dequantization literature (Chapter 4). Either way, it is a reason to look elsewhere.

## Reading the position once you own it

Owning a bookmark is not the same as reading it correctly. A few disciplines for the years between now and whenever the signal fires.

**Ignore the price action.** A pure-play quantum stock can double or halve in a quarter on sentiment, a press release, or a change in risk appetite across speculative tech broadly, while the underlying science moves not at all. The price is not measuring the thing you care about. Reading day-to-day moves as signal is the error the bookmark size is meant to immunize you against. If you find yourself watching the price daily, the position is probably too large.

**Track the technical metrics.** The numbers worth following are logical error rates, coherence times across full arrays, and gate fidelity at scale — the vocabulary of Chapter 2. When an earnings call or a filing mentions these, that is the paragraph to read carefully. When it does not, ask why. A company that can produce good technical numbers will produce them; one that cannot will talk about revenue and partnerships instead.

**Distinguish traction from advantage.** Revenue is traction. A government contract is traction. A partnership with a pharma company is traction. None of these is Signal 1, and none of them moves the four-part framework. Traction tells you the company is building something that customers are willing to pay to explore. Advantage tells you the hardware is doing something classically impossible. The two are not the same, and much of the narrative confusion in this sector comes from reading traction as advantage.

**Notice absence.** When a company announces a result without mentioning logical error rate, without specifying the active space of the molecule simulated, without naming the classical baseline — the absence of those specifics is itself information. A result stated in terms that cannot be evaluated is a press release, not a data point.

## What the position is actually for

Return to the paradox at the chapter's opening. You are a well-informed skeptic who understands why the breakthrough signal has not fired. Why own anything?

Because the signal will fire eventually, or it will not — and either outcome requires you to have been paying close attention across the years between now and then. If the signal fires and you missed it because you were not tracking the sector, you will react to a price move you do not understand, under time pressure, with an uninformed judgment. If the signal never fires, you want to know that too — and you want to know why, specifically, in the technical language you have been building. The bookmark position is how you stay in the conversation at the right level of depth.

The dollar amount is the price of the subscription. One to ten shares, or an amount you would genuinely not miss. Open it in the accessible names — IonQ today, Quantinuum when it prices, and whatever the ambient exposure in your index funds already handles for IBM and Alphabet. Apply the "fine if it goes to zero" test honestly. Do not average down on bad news until you have checked whether something fundamental changed. Do not size up until the four-part signal fires.

And then wait, and pay attention. That is the whole position.

## Further Reading

- **Daniel Kahneman, Olivier Sibony, and Cass Sunstein, *Noise: A Flaw in Human Judgment* (2021).** The clearest accessible treatment of the bias-versus-noise distinction that underlies the "fine if it goes to zero" test; the chapters on motivated reasoning explain precisely why a position large enough to hurt corrupts the judgments you need to make.
- **Nassim Taleb, *Fooled by Randomness* (2001).** The companion warning: a position sized to matter will trick you into reading random price action as meaningful signal, which in a sector as noise-saturated as quantum computing is a reliable path to bad decisions.
- **IonQ SEC filings and Quantinuum IPO prospectus (2025–2026).** The primary sources. Practice distinguishing revenue traction from scientific advantage in the language a company uses to describe its own results, before a third party verifies them.
